Hatred Between the Colonizers and the Natives in Shooting an Elephant

George Orwell’s Shooting an Elephant is a powerful criticism of British imperialism in Burma. In the essay, Orwell presents the painful relationship between the British colonizers and the Burmese natives. The Burmese hate the British because they are ruled, humiliated, and oppressed by a foreign power. On the other hand, the British officials develop hatred, contempt, and resentment toward the Burmese because of the constant hostility they face and because imperialism encourages racial superiority. Through his personal experience as a police officer, Orwell shows that colonialism creates hatred on both sides and makes both the rulers and the ruled morally unhappy.

First, Orwell clearly shows the hatred of the Burmese natives toward the British colonizers. The Burmese do not accept British rule willingly. They regard the British as foreign oppressors who have taken control of their country. Orwell describes how the natives insult him, laugh at him, and make his work as a police officer difficult. On the football field, for example, a Burmese player deliberately trips him, while the Burmese spectators laugh. Such incidents reveal their hostility toward the European rulers. Orwell also mentions that he was often subjected to insults and humiliating treatment. Their hatred is therefore a reaction to colonial domination.

The natives’ hatred is also connected with their loss of freedom and dignity. The British government controls Burma, and the Burmese have little power over their own country. Orwell understands that their hostility is not completely unreasonable. Although he personally dislikes the Burmese hostility, he recognizes that he is part of an unjust imperial system. His position as a police officer makes him a representative of British authority, even though he himself has doubts about imperialism.

Orwell also depicts the hatred and contempt of the colonizers toward the natives. The British officials often look down upon the Burmese and regard them as inferior. Orwell himself admits that he had developed a strong dislike for the Burmese. He hates their insults and their attempts to humiliate him. However, his hatred is complicated by his awareness that he is participating in an oppressive system. He does not simply present himself as an innocent victim; instead, he recognizes his own involvement in imperialism.

The most important point is that imperialism produces hatred even among the people who exercise power. Orwell says that he hates imperialism, but at the same time he hates the people who make his job difficult. This is one of the central ironies of the essay. As a British police officer, he is expected to maintain British authority, but he personally understands the injustice of colonial rule. Thus, he becomes trapped between his conscience and his official role.

The incident of the elephant brings this conflict to its climax. When Orwell finds that the elephant has stopped being dangerous, he no longer wants to kill it. He knows that killing the animal is unnecessary and that it would be morally wrong. However, a large crowd of Burmese people follows him and expects him to shoot. Orwell realizes that if he refuses, he will appear weak in front of the natives. Therefore, he shoots the elephant against his own judgment.

This incident symbolically represents the relationship between the colonizer and the colonized. The British appear powerful, but they are actually controlled by the expectations of the people they rule. Orwell explains that a colonial ruler must behave like a tyrant because otherwise he will lose authority. Thus, the colonizer becomes a prisoner of imperialism. The Burmese crowd, meanwhile, takes pleasure in the elephant’s death because they see the event partly as entertainment and partly as an opportunity to exercise power over the British officer.

The essay therefore presents a cycle of hatred and oppression. The British oppress the Burmese, and the Burmese respond with hatred and resistance. The British, in turn, become resentful and contemptuous of the Burmese. Neither side achieves genuine freedom or happiness. Orwell's own psychological conflict demonstrates that imperialism damages the humanity of both the ruler and the ruled.

The elephant itself can also be regarded as a symbol of imperialism. Like the elephant, Burma is forced into a condition of unnatural control. Orwell does not want to kill the elephant, just as he does not morally support imperialism, but the pressure of the colonial system forces him to act against his conscience. The unnecessary death of the elephant therefore reflects the unnecessary violence and suffering produced by imperial rule.

In conclusion, George Orwell depicts the relationship between the British colonizers and the Burmese natives as one characterized by mutual hatred, oppression, humiliation, and fear. The Burmese hate the British because they are deprived of freedom and treated as subjects, while the British develop hatred and contempt toward the natives because of their hostility and because colonialism encourages racial prejudice. Through the elephant episode, Orwell shows that imperialism is destructive not only to the colonized but also to the colonizer. Ultimately, Shooting an Elephant is a strong condemnation of imperialism and a profound portrayal of the hatred and psychological conflict created by colonial rule.
